# The science of food-delivery dispatch: how platforms pick couriers, and what courier behaviour measurably raises order flow

Scope note: this researcher covered the dispatch/matching literature and platform disclosures. Direct page fetches (arXiv, DoorDash, Grab, Fairwork PDFs) were blocked by the network proxy in this session, so findings below rest on search-engine extracts of those pages plus secondary coverage; where only an abstract or press summary was reachable, this is flagged. Every finding names platform and country. "AR" = acceptance rate, "CR" = cancellation/completion rate.

## Q1. What do published systems (Meituan, Ele.me, DoorDash, Uber Eats, Deliveroo, Grab, Gojek, foodpanda) say about the dispatch objective and the courier-side features that enter it?

### Takeaway
Every disclosed system is a repeated (every few seconds) global assignment that minimises a cost built from predicted delivery time / lateness, courier travel distance, courier waiting-at-merchant time and fulfilment, subject to a courier's current load and a promised delivery window; the courier-side inputs that are publicly admitted are location, current in-progress orders and their routes ("time slack"), the marginal detour the new order adds ("on-the-way-ness"), vehicle type, and (at Meituan) rider capability, weather and forecast demand. None of the Western or Chinese dispatch papers names AR or rating as a dispatch feature, but status/tier programs (DoorDash Platinum, Uber Pro, foodpanda "batch", Gojek incentives) sit on top of the optimiser and do gate priority or access.

- Meituan (China): allocation is not "one dispatch and done"; the platform keeps re-evaluating in-flight orders and offers "proactive reassignment" pop-ups when a rider is at timeout risk; in pilot cities riders who accepted reassignment cut their timeout rate by up to 51.79% — [Meituan news](https://www.meituan.com/news/NN25011005301105X)

### Inferences
- Across Meituan, DoorDash, Uber Eats, Deliveroo and Grab the common objective is a system-wide cost over all open orders (predicted lateness/ETA, courier travel, waiting at merchant, fulfilment within promised window), not "nearest courier". Grab says this explicitly ("doesn't always assign the nearest driver").
- The decisive courier-side variables that are publicly admitted are (a) projected arrival at the merchant relative to food-ready time, (b) marginal detour of the new order relative to the courier's existing route, (c) time slack on orders already carried, (d) vehicle suitability. These are all state variables a courier can influence in real time by where they are and what they already carry.
- Rating/AR enter not the optimiser but gating layers around it (DoorDash tier priority; Uber Pro; Gojek incentives; foodpanda batch, see Q2), so their effect is on which couriers see offers first, not on the per-offer cost.
- What this implies for one courier: be physically on, or very near, the merchants' side of the likely routes with spare time on whatever you already carry; the model is scoring "how little this order costs the system if you take it", and small detour + arrival at food-ready time is what scores well.

### Gaps
- No primary Swiggy, Zomato or Rappi dispatch documentation was found in this session; I found no reliable sources on their objective functions.
- No Meituan KDD proceedings paper could be confirmed by venue; the Meituan work retrieved is in Transportation Science (2025), arXiv (2024) and Tsinghua Sci. & Tech. (2023).
- Full text of the DoorDash DeepRed posts, the Meituan TS paper and the Grab explainers could not be fetched (proxy blocked), so exact weights, constraint lists and experiment tables are unverified.

## Q2. Does a courier's acceptance rate / decline history change future assignment probability? Which platforms admit it, which deny it, and what do audits and studies find?

### Takeaway
No platform publishes AR as a feature in the per-order optimiser, but several admit AR gates priority or access (DoorDash tier priority, Uber Pro/Eats Pro status, foodpanda batch ranking, Gojek incentives, Grab historical AR thresholds for incentives), while Deliveroo UK explicitly denies any penalty for rejecting. Independent evidence that score-based gating materially reduces work access comes from two Italian court rulings (Deliveroo Frank, Bologna 2020; Glovo/Foodinho, Palermo 2023) and a EUR 2.6m Garante fine; worker-side claims of "shadow bans" (Grab Cebu, Thailand protests) are consistent but unaudited.

### Inferences
- Two distinct mechanisms exist: (1) explicit tier/score gating (DoorDash Platinum priority, foodpanda batches, Glovo score, Deliveroo-Italy Frank ranking, Gojek incentives) where AR/attendance verifiably changes access to shifts or priority on offers; (2) the per-order optimiser, where no platform admits AR as a feature. Evidence for (1) is strong (court-tested); evidence that (2) silently penalises decliners is only anecdotal.
- A learned feature such as "probability this courier accepts" would be rational for a platform to include (the academic models above do exactly that, and DoorDash's secondhand-quoted scoring of offers before the MIP would be the natural place), but no platform discloses it and no audit has demonstrated it.
- Deliveroo UK's "no penalty" language and Uber's "affects Pro status" language are not contradictory: both decouple AR from the dispatch cost while coupling it to perks.
- What this implies for one courier: declines are unlikely to be "punished" inside the matching engine, but on platforms with tiers (and Grab historically used AR/CR thresholds for incentives) a low AR or high post-accept cancellation rate can cost priority, incentives, or shift access; cancelling after accepting is treated far more seriously than declining up front everywhere it is documented.

### Gaps
- No Grab Thailand or LINE MAN document stating whether AR/CR affects job allocation or tier was found; Thai sources only document protests about allocation and "ban" systems. Grab Thailand driver tiers/benefits could not be confirmed (results returned passenger GrabRewards tiers only).
- Worker Info Exchange's "Managed by Bots" report text was not reachable; only the campaign context around it was found.
- No independent quantitative audit (e.g., matched-courier experiment) measuring how much a given AR change alters offers per hour was found on any platform.

## Q3. Repositioning: wait near merchant clusters or near customers? Does moving while idle help? How big are the effects?

### Takeaway
The optimisation literature consistently finds that the value of a waiting location is its ability to reach many restaurants quickly (coverage radius dominates relocation distance), that platform-planned repositioning toward under-supplied zones improves efficiency and fairness, and that self-directed "cruising" toward perceived hot zones or chasing surge tends to hurt; the ride-hailing economics literature puts the gain from data-informed positioning at up to roughly 50% in simulation and finds parked waiting beats cruising.

### Cited Findings
- HBKU (Qatar, 2026), "Optimizing Courier Positioning and Demand Coverage in Online Food Delivery Platforms" (Tavasoli, Baldacci, Ghanbari): introduces the Dynamic Waiting Point Location Problem, a MIP jointly choosing waiting points, restaurant allocation, courier assignment and relocation over a horizon with coverage-radius, relocation-distance and capacity limits; "coverage radius emerges as the dominant factor in the solution, while relocation distance exhibits secondary effects"; partial-coverage variant outperformed full coverage with the same resources — [HBKU publication page](https://elmi.hbku.edu.qa/en/publications/optimizing-courier-positioning-and-demand-coverage-in-online-food/); press: "the ability of a waiting point to serve nearby restaurants had a stronger effect on demand coverage than allowing couriers to move longer distances between time periods" — [The Peninsula, June 2026](https://thepeninsulaqatar.com/article/14/06/2026/hbku-study-offers-fresh-insights-into-improving-efficiency-of-online-food-delivery)
- Cheng & Sharif Azadeh (2025), RL dual-control for dispatching and idle-courier steering, meal-delivery platform setting: models both order dispatching and courier steering as MDPs with predicted demand as input; policies reallocate idle couriers locally and report better delivery efficiency and fairness, with under-supplied areas improved — [arXiv 2501.05808](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.05808v1)
- Jahanshahi et al. (Knowledge-Based Systems 2022), deep RL for the meal delivery problem: studies order rejection as a lever to cut late orders and "intelligent repositioning during idle time"; including restaurant, customer and depot locations in the state improves expected reward and delivery times — [arXiv 2104.12000](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.12000v2); [ITU record](https://research.itu.edu.tr/en/publications/a-deep-reinforcement-learning-approach-for-the-meal-delivery-prob/)
- "Predict, Reposition, and Allocate" (2025): repositioning idle drivers toward forecast high-demand zones reduces downtime; larger relocation radii reduce travel time per order and raise driver profit in their simulation — [arXiv 2507.15282](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.15282)
- Erasmus University bachelor thesis (2022) on the meal-delivery routing problem: adding autonomous relocation (couriers moving on their own toward neighbourhoods they perceive as more profitable) lengthens delivery times — [Erasmus thesis](https://thesis.eur.nl/pub/63394/Thesis-2-.pdf) (student work)
- Drone + human-courier planning paper (2025): couriers modelled in dispatched, idle-cruising and idle-repositioning modes, with the platform steering repositioning via incentives toward zones with better earnings — [arXiv 2501.14325](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.14325)
- Boston University, WSDM 2018 (Chaudhari, Byers, Terzi), "Putting Data in the Driver's Seat" (Uber, NYC simulation): "repositioning throughout the day is key to maximizing driver earnings, whereas 'chasing surge' is typically misguided and sometimes a costly move"; dynamic-programming strategies evaluated on six months of NYC taxi + Uber API data — [BU DBLab project page](https://www.bu.edu/cs/research-groups/data-group/dblab/ride-hailing); secondary summary puts the gain at up to ~50% versus a naive driver — [The Morning Paper](https://blog.acolyer.org/2018/02/20/putting-data-in-the-drivers-seat-optimising-earnings-for-on-demand-ride-hailing/)
- Uber (US) internal city simulation: drivers who "just stay parked between trips ... make twice as much as those who drive around in search of passengers" — [VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/big-data/uber-simulated-a-city-to-teach-drivers-how-to-optimize-their-earnings)
- Cook, Diamond, Hall, List & Oyer (Uber, US; Review of Economic Studies 2021): a ~7% gender earnings gap among >1 million drivers is explained by experience on the platform (learning-by-doing), preferences over where to drive (driven largely by where drivers live and safety), and driving speed; timing-of-day preferences did not explain the gap — [Stanford GSB working paper page](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/working-papers/gender-earnings-gap-gig-economy-evidence-over-million-rideshare); [UChicago News](https://news.uchicago.edu/story/study-uncovers-gender-gap-earnings-uber-drivers)
- Uber (global): reports that AI positioning guidance "significantly accelerates the learning curve for new drivers" versus trial and error (company claim) — [Quantum Zeitgeist](https://quantumzeitgeist.com/uber-leverages-openai-simplify-earning/)
- Meituan (China): the dispatcher prefers riders for whom the new order adds the least distance to their current route ("顺路"), which mechanically favours riders already positioned between/near merchants on the active flow — [Guancha](https://www.guancha.cn/ChanJing/2021_11_05_613746.shtml)
- Empirical GPS pilot (US, 2018 data, preprint): 19 bike messengers/food couriers wore GPS watches; an algorithm classified idle periods between trips, but the study measured roadway exposure, not where idle time clusters relative to restaurants — [EasyChair preprint](https://easychair.org/publications/preprint/kNkT)
- Practitioner method (non-empirical): choosing waiting spots by overlapping OpenStreetMap restaurant buffers to maximise restaurants reachable within a radius — [Towards Data Science](https://towardsdatascience.com/optimising-the-location-of-food-delivery-couriers-using-geopandas-and-openstreetmap-e771433e5e9f/)

### Inferences
- The food-delivery-specific result (HBKU) and the Meituan "least added distance" rule point the same way: the best idle spot is the one from which the most high-volume merchants are reachable in the shortest time, i.e., merchant clusters, not customer residential areas (customers are only the second leg and are predicted by the model anyway).
- Platform-planned repositioning helps the system; self-directed cruising toward perceived hot zones is the one behaviour the modelling literature finds counter-productive, and the ride-hailing evidence (parked beats cruising; surge chasing loses) agrees. Moving is only worth it when it relocates you to a better coverage point before a peak, not during it.
- Effect sizes are from simulations: ~50% earnings uplift for an optimally positioned vs naive ride-hail driver, 2x for parked vs cruising; no food-delivery field experiment quantifies this for an individual courier.
- What this implies for one courier: pick a fixed waiting point that sits within a few minutes of the densest cluster of fast-turnover merchants for the coming meal window, arrive there before the peak starts, and stay put; do not drive around looking for pings and do not chase incentive zones once they appear.

### Gaps
- No empirical GPS study mapping where couriers actually idle relative to merchant clusters and linking that to orders received was found.
- Cook et al.'s exact experience premium (the oft-cited figure that drivers with >2,500 trips earn ~14% more per hour than those with <100) could not be verified from reachable sources in this session and is therefore not reported as a finding.
- No study specific to Bangkok or other Thai cities on repositioning.

## Q4. How do batching algorithms pick which courier gets the bundle, and does accepting batches lead to more batches?

### Takeaway
Bundles are formed by the same optimiser from orders that can all be delivered within their promised windows with minimal added detour, then assigned to the courier whose route and timing make the bundle cheapest (Meituan now explicitly learns bundling patterns from "skilled couriers"); batching intensity rises when supply is short or when customers opt into slower "Saver" tiers. No source gives evidence that a courier's history of accepting batches raises the probability of being offered future batches.

### Cited Findings
- Meituan (China): SCDN learns from how skilled couriers pool orders and uses graph representation learning to refine real-time many-to-one assignment; deployed; 45–55% courier-efficiency gain at the noon peak with timeliness maintained — [arXiv 2406.14635](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.14635v1)
- Meituan (China), Transportation Science 2025: strategic postponement of assignment (waiting to see whether a poolable order arrives) yields a 12% cost reduction in a Meituan-data simulation — [INFORMS TS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/trsc.2025.0129)
- Meituan (China): dispatch prefers the rider with more time slack on existing orders and the least added distance — which is the bundling criterion in practice — [Guancha](https://www.guancha.cn/ChanJing/2021_11_05_613746.shtml)
- DoorDash (US): the optimisation layer "makes decisions about batching orders, and strategically delays dispatches when necessary" — [DoorDash engineering](https://careersatdoordash.com/blog/using-ml-and-optimization-to-solve-doordashs-dispatch-problem/); journalistic account: the algorithm only batches orders that can be completed within the promised delivery window, considering prep times, driving distance and number of stops — [Marketing Scoop](https://www.marketingscoop.com/consumer/can-doordash-pick-up-multiple-orders/) (secondary)
- Uber Eats (global): deep neural networks "decide which orders are most efficient to batch together" — [Uber careers](https://www.uber.com/careers/list/144090)
- Grab (SEA): when supply is short the algorithm batches to fulfil as many requests as possible within the promised window; Saver orders are hyper-batched across outlets with routing that accounts for merchant waiting, traffic and weather — [Grab editorial](https://www.grab.com/sg/wp-json/wp/v2/editorial/197167); [Inside Grab Saver](https://www.grab.com/inside-grab/stories/how-the-saver-option-for-food-deliveries-works/)
- Grab (Thailand): batching capped at 2 orders per rider (Sept 2024 statement) — [Thairath](https://www.thairath.co.th/money/tech_innovation/tech_companies/2816229)
- Grab (SEA), MIT capstone: batching routes are built on deterministic time estimates without travel-time uncertainty — [MIT capstone](https://www.analyticscapstone.mit.edu/s/Grab_Gabriel_Afriat_Mariana_Suarez.pdf)
- Academic: bundling "enhances resource efficiency and cost-effectiveness, but it may result in additional detours and impact customer waiting times negatively" — [arXiv 2405.05620](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.05620); NeurADP adds batching and explicit courier assignment and beats myopic baselines — [arXiv 2311.12975](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.12975); Reyes et al. 2018 is the foundational meal-delivery routing model with bundling — [Optimization Online](https://optimization-online.org/?p=15189)
- Rider behaviour (Meituan data): riders act to keep an "optimal number of concurrent orders" — [TRID](https://trid.trb.org/View/2712805)
- Terminology caution: foodpanda's "batch" (HK/SG) is a rider priority tier set by AR/attendance, not an order bundle — [HKBU report](https://tyr-jour.hkbu.edu.hk/2022/11/21/foodpanda-riders-strike-over-wage-cuts/)

### Inferences
- The courier who gets a bundle is the one for whom the second order is nearly free: already heading to the same or an adjacent merchant, with slack on the first order. Being mid-pickup at a busy merchant during peak is therefore the state most likely to attract an add-on order (Meituan's postponement result says the platform deliberately waits for this).
- Supply shortage (peak, rain) is when batching rises, so couriers online in short-supply windows see more bundles regardless of their history.
- There is no documented memory of "accepts batches" in any optimiser; the only plausible link is indirect via tiers (DoorDash Platinum priority; foodpanda batch tiers) where accepting more of everything raises status.
- What this implies for one courier: to receive bundles, be the courier already at or heading to a high-volume merchant at peak with time slack; accepting a bundle helps per-hour earnings now but there is no evidence it changes future bundle probability.

### Gaps
- No platform or study addresses whether batch-acceptance history feeds future batch offers; treat any such claim as unsupported.
- Grab's actual bundling candidate-selection rule (e.g., max added minutes, max stops) is not published; the "2-order cap" is the only Thai-specific constraint found.

## Q5. Role of merchant food-preparation-time prediction in when and to whom an order is dispatched

### Takeaway
All major platforms predict prep time per merchant/order and dispatch so that the courier's predicted arrival coincides with food-ready time; this means orders are offered before food is ready, the optimiser penalises courier waiting at the merchant, and couriers whose projected arrival best matches the ready time (typically those nearby but not so near they would wait) are favoured; DoorDash and Meituan additionally hold ("postpone") dispatch when no good match exists yet.

### Cited Findings
- DoorDash (US): the dispatch objective includes "time waiting for an order to be ready"; order-ready times come from a prep-time model trained on censored data; the optimiser "strategically delays dispatches when necessary"; prep time varies by store cuisine and popularity — [DoorDash p=1350](https://careersatdoordash.com/?p=1350); [DoorDash dispatch post](https://careersatdoordash.com/blog/using-ml-and-optimization-to-solve-doordashs-dispatch-problem/); [DoorDash ETA post](https://careersatdoordash.com/blog/deep-learning-for-smarter-eta-predictions/)
- Uber Eats (global): "ideally, couriers should arrive at the restaurant the moment food is ready"; restaurants lack incentive to report prep time, so it is modelled from history plus time of day, day of week, order size, location — [InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/articles/uber-eats-time-predictions); [QCon 2019 slides](https://archive.qconnewyork.com/system/files/presentation-slides/final_version_of_qcon_nyc_2019.pdf); courier waiting-at-restaurant state is inferred and "can inform dispatch decisions" — [Uber blog](https://www.uber.com/blog/uber-eats-trip-optimization/)
- Grab (SEA): the engine computes when the partner should leave so they arrive as food is ready, using travel time plus prep-time prediction; Bluetooth beacons at outlets have been used to measure prep time — [Grab editorial 197167](https://www.grab.com/sg/wp-json/wp/v2/editorial/197167)
- Deliveroo (UK): Frank estimates each restaurant's prep time from order type and time of day and uses it with rider travel-time predictions — [Silicon Republic](https://siliconrepublic.com/machines/deliveroo-algorithm-frank-machine-learning); [Camden document](https://camdocs.camden.gov.uk/CMWebDrawer/Record/9280611/file/document)
- Meituan (China): merchant food-ready time (商家出餐) is an explicit dispatch input — [CSDN repost of Meituan tech](https://blog.csdn.net/jinjin603/article/details/78793243); strategic postponement of assignment cut cost 12% — [INFORMS TS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/trsc.2025.0129)
- Ele.me/Alibaba (China): restaurant busyness proxied by orders waiting for a rider; busy restaurants get longer fulfilment-time estimates — [Alibaba Cloud](https://www.alibabacloud.com/en/developer/a/ai/fulfillment-time-estimation?_p_lc=1)
- CloudKitchens (US): prep estimates from delivery companies used to be fixed manual values (0/5/10 min) regardless of kitchen load; their model uses prep-state transitions to beat those estimates — [CloudKitchens tech blog](https://techblog.cloudkitchens.com/p/food-prep-time-prediction)
- US patent: deep-learning system for dynamic prediction of order preparation times notes that delivery-time estimates "depend primarily on the order preparation times provided ... by restaurants" — [USPTO 12518131](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/12518131)

### Inferences
- Because courier waiting at the merchant is a cost term and arrival-at-ready-time is the target, the optimiser's preferred courier for a given order is the one whose travel time to the merchant approximately equals remaining prep time — "near enough" rather than "nearest". For fast merchants (short prep) this collapses to nearest; for slow merchants a courier a few minutes away who is finishing another drop can be preferred.
- Dispatch postponement means an order can sit unassigned for seconds to minutes waiting for a courier to become a cheap match; a courier who becomes free (completes a drop) close to a merchant with a pending order is often the one who receives it.
- What this implies for one courier: complete drops quickly and mark them promptly so the system sees you as free near merchants; waiting outside merchants with long, unpredictable prep times is less valuable than waiting near clusters of fast-prep merchants where "nearest" is favoured.

### Gaps
- No platform publishes the tolerance (minutes early/late) it targets for courier arrival vs ready time.
- No Thai-platform prep-time methodology was found (LINE MAN, foodpanda TH, Robinhood).

## Q6. Weather, time-of-day, day-of-week and payday effects on order volume per courier (esp. Southeast Asia / Thailand)

### Takeaway
Platform-data studies (all from China) show order volume is overwhelmingly driven by hour of day (hourly range >4x the mean, peaks ~11–12 and ~18–19), day-of-week matters much less, and rain/snow/heat raise orders (e.g. lunch orders +12.6% at 35°C vs 20°C); for Thailand only Grab press data exist (11:00–12:00 lunch peak under subsidy schemes; >70% of night orders between 21:00 and midnight). No payday-cycle evidence was found anywhere.

### Cited Findings
- China (platform order records, mixed-methods study): dispatch volume is highly hour-dependent — the range of average deals across hours exceeds 4.4x the mean; volume climbs sharply to a peak around 11–12, dips until ~15–16, then rises to a second peak around 18–19; day-of-week variance is small by comparison — [arXiv 2208.04518](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2208.04518)
- China (100 cities, Nature Cities / Tsinghua): lunchtime delivery orders rise by more than 12.6% as temperature goes from 20°C to 35°C and by 21.4% at 40°C; dinner orders rise far less — [Tsinghua FI publication PDF](https://fi.ee.tsinghua.edu.cn/public/publications/fd416970-ecd1-11ef-857b-0242ac120006.pdf)
- China (Beijing, Harbin, Hangzhou; one platform): rain, snow and air quality significantly affect delivery sales with city-specific effects; weekends and pre/post-holiday periods also matter — [HIT scholar page](https://scholar.hit.edu.cn/en/publications/weather-and-time-factors-impact-on-online-food-delivery-sales-a-c/); [Springer, Theoretical and Applied Climatology 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00704-023-04542-w)
- China (Meituan data, Kybernetes 2022): temperature, air quality and rainfall have clear effects on most takeaway categories — [Unpaywall record](https://unpaywall.org/10.1108%2FK-05-2020-0322)
- China (HBR, Tongji SEM, "When Gig Work Meets Extreme Weather"): extreme weather raises demand on couriers while raising their risk — [Tongji SEM](https://sem.tongji.edu.cn/semen/?p=25714)
- Grab (Thailand, 2026 "Thais Help Thais Plus 60/40" co-payment scheme): 55% of orders were 100–250 THB and the 11:00–12:00 lunch hour was the peak usage time; the earlier "Kon La Krueng Plus" scheme showed the same 11:00–12:00 peak (subsidy-driven, may not reflect normal demand) — [Grab TH press 60/40](https://www.grab.com/th/en/press/others/6040_campaignimpatcs/); [Grab TH press 50/50 Plus](https://www.grab.com/th/en/press/others/grab5050plusimpact/)
- Grab (Thailand) night-time insights release (undated): more than 70% of food-delivery orders at night are placed between 21:00 and midnight; top night dish Som Tum Pu Pla Ra — [Grab TH nighttime insights](https://www.grab.com/th/en/press/others/nighttimeinsights/)
- LINE MAN (Thailand): the company explained a viral post about a "temporary special fee" used to stimulate riders to accept jobs (an on-demand incentive responding to supply shortfall) — [The Thaiger TH](https://thethaiger.com/th/news/1357428/)
- US restaurant direct-ordering vendor data (4M+ orders, Mar 2025–Mar 2026, not gig platform): Friday strongest, Thursday/Saturday next, Monday weakest; dinner 17:30–20:30 main peak, lunch 11:00–13:00 secondary — [Restolabs](https://www.restolabs.com/blog/online-food-delivery-statistics)
- Cambodia (ILO): platform delivery workers worked 6.7–6.8 days/week and 11–12 hours/day including unpaid idle time waiting for requests — [eTrade for all / ILO Cambodia study](https://etradeforall.org/news/digital-labour-platforms-cambodia-offer-opportunities-businesses-and-workers-despite)

### Inferences
- Orders-per-online-courier is demand divided by online supply; the Chinese hourly data (4.4x range) imply that being online outside the two meal peaks yields a small fraction of peak order flow regardless of positioning. Rain and heat raise demand and (per Grab's own batching rule) trigger batching, so bad-weather peaks are the highest-yield windows if the courier is willing to ride in them.
- For Thailand the only platform-published temporal facts are a sharp 11:00–12:00 lunch peak and a 21:00–24:00 night concentration (Grab); day-of-week and payday effects are undocumented.
- What this implies for one courier: concentrate online hours on 11:00–13:00 and 17:30–20:30 (and, on Grab Thailand, 21:00–24:00 for night orders), prioritise rainy and very hot days, and expect off-peak hours to yield only a fraction of peak volume whatever you do.

### Gaps
- No Southeast-Asian or Thai study quantifying rain or temperature effects on order volume was found; Thai-language searches returned no statistics.
- No evidence on payday / end-of-month cycles in food-delivery demand on any platform.
- No Grab/LINE MAN/foodpanda Thailand data on day-of-week volume.

## Q7. Studies specifically on Thai delivery platforms (Grab, LINE MAN, foodpanda, Robinhood) and rider earnings/algorithms

### Inferences
- Thai riders' order flow is heavily shaped by platform-level decisions (incentive cuts by province, allocation-system changes, 2-order batching cap) that no individual can control, and Thai sources document no stable published tier/AR rule for Grab or LINE MAN; the practical levers for a Thai courier are therefore the universal ones from Q1–Q6 (positioning near merchant clusters, peak/rain hours, keeping post-accept cancellations low) rather than any Thailand-specific trick.
- The 41.2% of protests citing "algorithm malfunctions" and the recurring "ban system" complaints indicate riders perceive allocation suppression, but no Thai audit has measured it.
- What this implies for one courier in Thailand: treat your effective hourly rate (Fairwork: often below minimum wage when all online hours are counted) as the metric, and raise it by compressing online hours into peaks at merchant clusters rather than extending hours.
